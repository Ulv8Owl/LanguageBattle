1.1 "I've been working" — Present Perfect Continuous: the action started in the past and is still going on now.
1.2 "in the same team" — "The same" always takes "the". "In a team", not "at a team".
1.3 "for three years" — "For" + a length of time. Use "since" for a starting point: since 2023.
1.4 "Recently" — It typically goes with a perfect tense and means "in the last short period".
1.5 "I've started" — Present Perfect for a change whose result is true now.
1.6 "to feel a bit stuck" — "Start to feel" and "start feeling" are both correct. "A bit" softens the negative word "stuck".
1.7 "My manager" — In British companies this is the normal neutral word; "boss" is more informal.
1.8 "suggested a short course" — After "suggest" never use "to + verb": suggest a course / suggest doing.
1.9 ", and I signed up last week" — "Sign up" = register for something. It is separable: sign up for a course.
1.10 "If it goes well" — First conditional: after "if" we use the present, not "will".
1.11 "I might apply" — "Might" = maybe; weaker and safer than "will".
1.12 "for a different role" — "Apply for" a job. "Role" is the usual office word for "position".
2.1 "We moved" — Past Simple for a finished event with a clear date.
2.2 "into a new flat" — "Move into" a home; "move to" a city. "Flat" is British, "apartment" American.
2.3 "in April" — "In" + month.
2.4 "The building" — "The" because it is the building of our new flat.
2.5 "is old" — Present Simple: it is still true.
2.6 ", so things break quite often" — "Quite often" = fairly often, not very often. "So" gives the result.
2.7 "Last month" — Past time phrase without a preposition.
2.8 "the heating" — Uncountable and British; Americans say "the heat".
2.9 "stopped working for a week" — After "stop", "-ing" means the action ended. "Stop to work" would mean "pause in order to work".
2.10 "The landlord" — The person who owns the flat you rent.
2.11 "fixed it in the end" — "In the end" = finally, after problems. Do not confuse it with "at the end (of the film)".
2.12 ", but it took far too long" — "Far too" is stronger than "too": much more than acceptable.
3.1 "I've been learning Spanish" — Perfect Continuous again: from the past up to now, and still going.
3.2 "for about a year" — "About" = approximately; you can also say "for roughly a year".
3.3 ", mostly on my phone" — "Mostly" = in most cases. "On my phone" is the fixed preposition.
3.4 "I can read" — After "can" the verb has no "to".
3.5 "menus and short articles" — Plural with no article: things in general.
3.6 "without much trouble" — "Much" is used with uncountable nouns in negative-sounding phrases.
3.7 "Speaking" — The -ing form used as a subject (a gerund).
3.8 "is still the hard part" — "Still" = the situation has not changed. "The hard part" is a very common collocation.
3.9 ", especially on the phone" — "Especially" adds the strongest example. "On the phone" = during a call.
3.10 "Next summer" — Future time with no preposition after "next".
3.11 "I'd like to spend" — "Would like to" is a polite, softer way of saying "want to".
3.12 "two weeks in Valencia" — "Spend time in a place" is the standard pattern.
4.1 "Our flight" — "Flight" = the journey by plane, not the plane itself.
4.2 "was delayed" — Passive voice: we do not say who caused the delay.
4.3 "by four hours" — "By" + the size of a difference: delayed by four hours.
4.4 "Nobody" — Already negative, so the verb stays positive: nobody could, not "nobody couldn't".
4.5 "at the desk" — "At" for a service point: at the desk, at the counter.
4.6 "could tell us why" — An indirect question keeps normal word order: why it was delayed, not "why was it".
4.7 "In the end" — Finally, after a long wait. The same phrase as in line 2.
4.8 "they gave us" — "They" here means the airline staff, without naming them.
4.9 "a voucher for a sandwich" — "A voucher for" + what you can get with it. The tone is quietly ironic: four hours, one sandwich.
4.10 "I've decided" — Present Perfect: the decision was made recently and still holds.
4.11 "to fly with someone else" — "Fly with + an airline" is the standard collocation.
4.12 "next time" — Future expression with no preposition.
5.1 "I used to play" — "Used to" = a past habit that has now stopped.
5.2 "the guitar" — Musical instruments take "the" after "play": play the guitar, play the piano.
5.3 "when I was at school" — "At school" = while I was a pupil, not inside the building.
5.4 "I gave it up" — "Give up" = stop doing something. With a pronoun it must be split: give it up.
5.5 "when I started working" — After "start" both "-ing" and "to + verb" are possible.
5.6 ", and I regretted it later" — "Regret + noun/-ing" for a past action; "regret to say" is a different, formal pattern.
5.7 "A friend of mine" — Natural English for "one of my friends". "A my friend" is impossible.
5.8 "lent me his old one" — "Lend" = give temporarily; "borrow" = take temporarily. "One" replaces "guitar".
5.9 "in January" — "In" + month.
5.10 "Now" — It marks the contrast with "used to" at the beginning.
5.11 "I practise" — British spelling of the verb; the noun is "practice". American English uses "practice" for both.
5.12 "twenty minutes every evening" — Duration + frequency, with no preposition before either.
6.1 "My sister" — A family word; no article with the possessive.
6.2 "has just finished" — "Just" + Present Perfect = a very short time ago.
6.3 "her first year at university" — "At university" without an article is British usage for studying there.
6.4 "She's studying" — Present Continuous for a temporary situation that continues over months.
6.5 "something with data" — Deliberately vague; "data" is normally treated as uncountable in everyday English.
6.6 ", which I don't really understand" — "Which" refers to the whole idea before it. "Don't really" is softer than "really don't".
6.7 "Whenever I ask" — "Whenever" = every time that. Present tense for a repeated situation.
6.8 "she explains it" — "Explain" needs an object: explain it to me, never "explain me".
6.9 "very patiently" — Adverb from "patient"; adjectives in "-t" simply add "-ly".
6.10 "I still" — "Still" between the subject and the verb keeps the sentence natural.
6.11 "have no idea" — Fixed phrase: "have no idea" = understand nothing at all.
6.12 ", but I'm proud of her anyway" — "Proud of" + person. "Anyway" at the end = in spite of that.
7.1 "We were supposed to launch" — "Be supposed to" = it was the plan, but it did not happen.
7.2 "the new website" — "The" because it is a specific project both speakers know.
7.3 "in June" — "In" + month.
7.4 "Then the designer" — "Then" moves the story forward in time.
7.5 "left the company" — "Leave a company" = stop working there. No preposition needed.
7.6 ", and everything stopped for a month" — "For" + how long the pause lasted.
7.7 "We finally" — "Finally" = after a long delay; it goes before the main verb.
7.8 "went live" — Work idiom: "go live" = become available to users.
7.9 "last Thursday" — "Last" + day, no preposition.
7.10 "So far" — "So far" = up to now, and things may still change.
7.11 "nobody has complained" — Present Perfect for the period that includes now. "Nobody" is already negative.
7.12 ", which already feels like a win" — "Feel like" + noun. The understatement is the joke: no complaints counts as success.
8.1 "I try" — Present Simple for a repeated effort.
8.2 "to keep a budget" — "Keep a budget" = record and control your spending.
8.3 ", but it never quite works" — "Quite" after a negative = "not completely". This is British softening.
8.4 "The problem" — "The" because we are naming one specific problem.
8.5 "isn't the big things" — "Things" is plural, but "the problem" is the subject, so the verb stays singular.
8.6 ", it's the small ones" — "Ones" replaces the plural noun "things".
8.7 "Coffee" — Uncountable here: the habit of buying coffee.
8.8 "and taxis" — Countable and plural: taxi rides in general.
8.9 "add up faster than you think" — "Add up" = become a large total. "You" here means "people in general".
8.10 "This month" — A period that includes now, so a perfect tense follows.
8.11 "I've written down" — "Write down" = record on paper. Irregular: write → written.
8.12 "every single payment" — "Single" adds emphasis: absolutely all of them, without exception.
9.1 "I don't sleep well" — "Well" is the adverb; "I don't sleep good" is incorrect.
9.2 "if I look at my laptop" — In an "if" clause about a general rule, use the present tense.
9.3 "late in the evening" — "Late in the evening" is natural; "in the late evening" is not.
9.4 "I've tried" — Present Perfect for an experiment made at some point before now.
9.5 "leaving it" — "Try + -ing" = experiment with a method. "Try to leave" would mean it was difficult.
9.6 "in the kitchen" — "In" + room.
9.7 "It works" — "Work" here = be effective, not "do a job".
9.8 ", but only" — "Only" placed here prepares a strong limit on what follows.
9.9 "when I actually do it" — "Actually" = in reality, not "at the moment". The self-irony is the point.
9.10 "Habits" — Plural with no article = habits in general.
9.11 "are much easier to plan" — "Adjective + to + verb" pattern; "much" strengthens the comparative.
9.12 "than to keep" — The structure must stay parallel: "to plan" ... "to keep".
10.1 "My old car" — Two-word description before the noun: possessive + adjective.
10.2 "finally" — After a long process; it hints that this was expected.
10.3 "died in February" — "Die" used about a machine is informal and slightly humorous.
10.4 "Instead of buying another one" — After "instead of" always use "-ing". "Another one" = a different car.
10.5 "I decided" — Past Simple: a single finished decision.
10.6 "to try the train" — After "decide" we use "to + verb".
10.7 "The journey" — British word for the trip from A to B; Americans often say "the commute" for daily travel.
10.8 "takes longer" — "Take" + time. "Longer" is the comparative of "long".
10.9 ", but I can read on the way" — "On the way" = while travelling.
10.10 "I don't think" — English moves the negative to "think": "I don't think I'll go" is normal, "I think I won't go" sounds odd.
10.11 "I'll go back" — "Go back to" = return to a previous habit.
10.12 "to driving" — Here "to" is a preposition, so the "-ing" form follows.
