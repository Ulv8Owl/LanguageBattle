1.1 "The reorganisation was announced" — Agentless passive in the opening clause: the decision arrives without an author, which is precisely how it felt.
1.2 "on a Tuesday" — "A Tuesday" rather than "Tuesday" makes the day generic and turns it into evidence for a rule.
1.3 ", which is when nothing good is ever announced" — A sentential relative clause defining a day by what happens on it. "Ever" after a negative subject gives it the weight of folk law.
1.4 "We were promised" — The double passive of "promise somebody something"; the promiser is again unnamed.
1.5 "fewer layers" — "Fewer" with countable nouns. "Layers" is management jargon for levels of hierarchy.
1.6 "and faster decisions" — Two abstract nouns delivered as a slogan; the line will spend the next five sentences testing them.
1.7 "Six months on" — "On" as an adverb of elapsed time; more idiomatic and more clipped than "six months later".
1.8 ", we have both" — The pronoun keeps the promise intact just long enough for the next clause to spoil it.
1.9 ", and also two new committees" — The "and also" is deliberately clumsy, as if the fact were an afterthought. The committees quietly undo "fewer layers".
1.10 "My own job" — "Own" after a possessive is intensifying, not redundant; it narrows the focus from the company to the speaker.
1.11 "is technically unchanged" — "Technically" concedes the formal truth while withdrawing belief in it — the same move as in the C1 set, at higher stakes.
1.12 ", which nobody quite believes" — "Quite" with a negative means "not completely". English politeness lives in this gap.
1.13 "I now report" — "Report to" is fixed managerial usage; the position of "now" marks the change without dramatising it.
1.14 "to someone" — The indefinite pronoun refuses to name the person and so keeps the criticism structural.
1.15 "who reports to my previous manager" — The repetition of the verb builds the chain the sentence is quietly counting.
1.16 "In fairness" — A concession marker; placing it here prevents the whole line from curdling into complaint.
1.17 ", she's very good" — Deliberately plain praise. The absence of qualification signals sincerity in a paragraph made of qualifications.
1.18 ", and the meetings are shorter" — Evidence rather than assertion; it also restores balance before the conclusion.
1.19 "Whether any of this" — A "whether" clause as subject: formal, judicial, and slightly parodic in a sentence about committees.
1.20 "counts as fewer layers" — "Count as" tests a claim against a definition, which is exactly the manoeuvre being satirised.
1.21 "depends on how you count" — The pun on "counts / count" closes the line. It answers nothing and settles everything.
2.1 "The hardest thing" — A superlative frame that promises a thesis; the whole line is built to earn it.
2.2 "about speaking a foreign language well" — "About" introduces the domain of the superlative. "Well" is the load-bearing word.
2.3 "is that people stop making allowances" — "Make allowances for somebody" = judge them less strictly. The idiom, not the grammar, is the difficulty here.
2.4 "At B1" — "At" + a level, used the way one says "at sea level": a position, not an achievement.
2.5 "everyone was delighted" — "Delighted" is a strong adjective used for a trivial cause; the mismatch is the irony.
2.6 "that I could order a coffee" — After "delighted that", the clause takes a normal finite verb. The banality of the example is the argument.
2.7 "Somewhere after that" — Vague time reference imitating memory rather than record-keeping.
2.8 "the compliments stopped" — Intransitive "stop" with an abstract subject: the change happened to the speaker, not because of him.
2.9 ", and the corrections began" — Perfectly parallel to the previous clause. The symmetry is what makes the loss feel like a promotion.
2.10 "This is progress" — A flat declarative sentence dropped between two ironic ones; the shift in texture marks sincerity.
2.11 ", though it rarely feels like it" — "Feel like it" refers back to "progress" with a pronoun, which keeps the register conversational.
2.12 "at the time" — A fixed phrase meaning "in the moment it happens", as opposed to in hindsight.
2.13 "The last ten per cent" — A borrowed engineering proportion applied to language learning; the metaphor does the analytical work.
2.14 "is not vocabulary" — The negative half of the definition, set up for the semicolon.
2.15 "; it's knowing when not to be clever" — The semicolon replaces "but" and tightens the antithesis. "Not to be clever" is the crucial infinitive negation.
2.16 "I still overshoot" — "Overshoot" comes from ballistics; in speech it means using more than the moment required.
2.17 ", usually with a word" — "With" marks the instrument of the error; "usually" makes it a pattern rather than an anecdote.
2.18 "I learned that morning" — A contact relative clause. "That morning" is the detail that makes the confession specific and therefore credible.
2.19 "Fluency" — Abstract noun with no article, fronted as the topic of the closing sentence.
2.20 ", as far as I can tell" — Epistemic hedge; it lowers the volume of the aphorism just before it lands.
2.21 ", is mostly restraint" — A one-word definition that reverses the usual meaning of fluency. "Mostly" keeps it arguable, which is why it persuades.
3.1 "I have now spent" — Present Perfect with "now": the total is being counted up to this moment, and it is still rising.
3.2 "more hours in that tax office" — "That" rather than "the" carries fatigue; it assumes the listener has heard about this before.
3.3 "than in most of my friends' homes" — The comparison must stay parallel ("in... than in..."). The plural possessive apostrophe follows the s.
3.4 "Each visit" — "Each" individualises where "every" would generalise; it prepares the loop described next.
3.5 "requires a document" — The impersonal verb keeps the bureaucracy faceless and therefore inevitable.
3.6 "that only exists after the previous visit" — A defining relative clause describing a circular rule. Nothing is exaggerated; the structure is the joke.
3.7 "The woman at desk four" — Precise, unnamed, institutional. The number is what makes her real.
3.8 "has developed" — The same verb one uses for symptoms and habits; applied to sympathy, it suggests something involuntary.
3.9 "a certain sympathy for me" — "A certain" signals a quantity the speaker will not specify. "Sympathy for" rather than "with" keeps it slightly distant.
3.10 "She no longer asks" — "No longer" is more formal and more final than "not any more".
3.11 "why I'm there" — Embedded question with statement word order, and the present tense for a recurring truth.
3.12 "; she simply reaches for the right stamp" — The semicolon links cause and consequence without a conjunction. "Simply" and "the right stamp" together do all the characterisation.
3.13 "Last week" — A definite past marker that will collide with the perfect tense in the next sentence.
3.14 "she told me" — "Tell somebody something", as opposed to "say something to somebody".
3.15 "I was almost finished" — Backshift in reported speech, with "finished" as an adjective describing a state.
3.16 "I have heard that before" — Present Perfect for accumulated experience; the flatness implies a long history of the same sentence.
3.17 ", and I have learned" — The repeated perfect builds a parallel that turns anecdote into philosophy.
3.18 "not to celebrate early" — Negative infinitive: "not" goes before "to". "Early" here means "prematurely".
3.19 "Still" — A one-word concessive; positioned alone, it turns the whole line towards warmth.
3.20 ", there's something oddly comforting" — "Something + adjective" postpones the noun and admits the feeling is hard to name. "Oddly" pre-empts the reader's objection.
3.21 "about a bureaucracy that at least remembers your face" — "At least" concedes the minimum; the generic "your" invites the reader into the scene, and the sentence ends on the one human detail in the whole system.
4.1 "The best manager I ever had" — A superlative with a contact relative clause; "ever" quietly rules out any competition.
4.2 "said almost nothing" — "Almost nothing" is a stronger characterisation than "little", and it sets up a paradox the line then resolves.
4.3 "in meetings" — Plural with no article: the general context, not a particular occasion.
4.4 "She would let" — "Would" for a characteristic past habit — more elegant here than "used to", which would sound merely nostalgic.
4.5 "the loud people" — A nominalised adjective doing the work of a whole description; the register is affectionately dismissive.
4.6 "finish" — The bare infinitive after "let". No "to" is possible.
4.7 "Then she'd ask" — The contracted "she'd" keeps the narrative pace conversational.
4.8 "one question" — "One" instead of "a" emphasises singularity and economy of means.
4.9 "that quietly rearranged the room" — "Rearrange the room" is metonymy: the furniture stands for the people and their positions.
4.10 "It took me years" — The "it takes somebody time to do something" pattern, with the delay itself as the subject.
4.11 "to realise" — The infinitive completes the pattern; British spelling "realise", American "realize".
4.12 "that this was a technique, not a temperament" — Two abstract nouns in antithesis, separated by a comma rather than "but" — a compressed and slightly aphoristic construction.
4.13 "I've tried it" — Present Perfect for lifetime experience with no specified time.
4.14 "perhaps four times" — "Perhaps" with a number is a very English way of being precise about being imprecise.
4.15 ", and on three occasions it worked" — Fronting the adverbial makes the arithmetic audible before the verdict.
4.16 "The fourth time" — The definite article picks out the remaining case; no preposition is needed.
4.17 "I asked the question too early" — "The question" refers back to the one already defined; "too early" is the whole diagnosis.
4.18 ", and it just sounded rude" — "Just" limits the effect to what was perceived, and "sounded" locates the failure in the hearer, not the intention.
4.19 "Timing" — A gerund promoted to abstract noun and fronted for weight.
4.20 ", it turns out" — A parenthetical discovery marker, inserted mid-sentence to slow the conclusion down.
4.21 ", is the part nobody can teach you" — "The part" keeps the register modest; "nobody can teach you" closes the essay-in-miniature without a moral.
5.1 "My mother" — The plainest possible opening; the restraint is the tone of the whole line.
5.2 "has started telling me stories" — "Start + -ing" for a gradual change. Present Perfect keeps it current.
5.3 "I already know" — A contact relative clause; "already" carries a faint impatience that the next sentence will correct.
5.4 "For a while" — A vague duration that implies the behaviour ended.
5.5 "I corrected her" — Past Simple, brief and unadorned; the brevity is the confession.
5.6 ", which helped nobody" — A sentential relative delivering a verdict on the speaker's own behaviour. "Nobody" is deliberately absolute.
5.7 "Now I listen" — The pivot of the line; "now" opposes "for a while" directly.
5.8 "for the small differences" — "Listen for" means to listen with a purpose, unlike "listen to". The whole change of attitude is inside that preposition.
5.9 ", and there are always some" — Existential "there are" plus "some" without a noun; the understatement leaves the discovery to the reader.
5.10 "The version she told last spring" — Contact relative again, and "version" quietly introduces the idea that a story has editions.
5.11 "had a different ending" — Past Simple inside a description of a particular telling.
5.12 "from the one I grew up with" — "Different from" is the safest form in careful English; the preposition stays at the end of the relative clause.
5.13 "I have decided" — Present Perfect for a resolution that still governs the speaker's behaviour.
5.14 "that both are true" — A statement that would be illogical anywhere except here, which is why the next element is needed.
5.15 "in the way that matters" — A restrictive clause narrowing the claim to one dimension, and refusing to specify which.
5.16 "She's not losing the story" — Present Continuous for a process, negated to reject an interpretation the reader may have formed.
5.17 "; she's still deciding" — The semicolon holds the correction against the rejection. "Still" implies the process began long ago.
5.18 "what it means" — Embedded question with statement word order; the shortest possible ending to that clause.
5.19 "I suspect" — Hedging that lets the speaker say something certain without claiming certainty.
5.20 "that in about thirty years" — The approximation is the joke and the tenderness at once.
5.21 "I'll be doing exactly the same" — Future Continuous for an ongoing state at a future point; "exactly" removes the last trace of superiority.
6.1 "The outage lasted" — "Outage" is the standard technical noun; "last" takes a duration without a preposition.
6.2 "eleven minutes" — The precise, small number is the whole setup.
6.3 ", which we will be discussing for eleven months" — Future Continuous plus the repeated numeral: the parallel between "minutes" and "months" is the joke, and it needs no comment.
6.4 "The cause" — A single definite noun; incident reports speak this way, and the register is borrowed intact.
6.5 "was a configuration change" — Compound noun, singular, deliberately undramatic.
6.6 "nobody remembers approving" — A reduced relative clause with a gerund: "remember + -ing" refers to recalling a past act, unlike "remember to".
6.7 "The post-mortem" — Medical vocabulary long since naturalised in engineering; it still carries a faint absurdity.
6.8 "was blameless" — "Blameless post-mortem" is a fixed term of art in the industry, which the next clause immediately dismantles.
6.9 ", in the sense that everyone knew whose it was" — "In the sense that" restricts the meaning of the term; "whose it was" ends on a possessive pronoun, the shortest way to name a culprit without naming one.
6.10 "We produced" — "Produce" for documents rather than results; the verb is already an admission.
6.11 "fourteen action items" — "Action item" is meeting jargon. The number sets up the arithmetic.
6.12 ", of which we completed the two easy ones" — Formal relative structure with "of which"; "the two easy ones" lands the punchline in plain words.
6.13 "In our defence" — A formal concessive opener that signals a defence which is about to fail.
6.14 ", the other twelve" — Ellipsis of "action items"; the number carries the noun.
6.15 "would have required admitting something structural" — Third conditional without an "if" clause, plus a gerund after "require". "Structural" is the euphemism the whole line has been building towards.
6.16 "The system" — The definite article returns the focus to the object of all this activity.
6.17 "is now marginally safer" — "Marginally" is a technical-sounding adverb used to make a tiny gain sound audited.
6.18 "and considerably more complicated" — The asymmetry between "marginally" and "considerably" is the argument.
6.19 "I'd call that" — "Would" for a considered judgement, as in the C1 set.
6.20 "an honest description" — The praise is for the description, not the outcome — a distinction the sentence relies on.
6.21 "of most engineering" — The generalisation at the end enlarges the anecdote into a claim, and stops just short of proving it.
7.1 "I used to finish" — "Used to" for a discontinued habit, which announces from the start that the principle has been abandoned.
7.2 "every book I started" — "Every" with a singular noun and a contact relative clause; the compression suits the briskness of the confession.
7.3 ", on principle" — A fixed adverbial meaning "as a matter of principle". It is set up here purely to be examined.
7.4 "The principle" — The noun is repeated as a subject so the sentence can put it on trial.
7.5 "turned out to be" — "Turn out" reveals a truth discovered after the fact; the pattern needs "to be".
7.6 "mostly vanity" — A blunt abstract noun with no article. "Mostly" prevents it from sounding like a performance of humility.
7.7 "Now I abandon them" — "Abandon" is stronger than "give up" and slightly theatrical, which is the point.
7.8 "freely" — A single adverb carrying the new philosophy.
7.9 ", and I read considerably more" — The comparative with no "than" clause; the second term is understood from the first sentence.
7.10 "There's a particular pleasure" — Existential opener introducing an abstraction; "particular" promises precision that the next clause delivers.
7.11 "in closing a book" — After "pleasure in", the gerund is obligatory.
7.12 "you were supposed to admire" — "Be supposed to" for social obligation; the generic "you" spreads the guilt to the reader.
7.13 "It's the same pleasure" — The sentence turns to analogy, the classic move of the essayist.
7.14 "as leaving a party" — "The same... as" is the correct correlative pair; a gerund follows "as".
7.15 "at exactly the right moment" — "Exactly" placed before "the right" is idiomatic; the phrase names a skill nobody can define.
7.16 "My shelves" — Metonymy: the shelves stand for the self one displays.
7.17 "have become less impressive" — Present Perfect for a change with a visible present result.
7.18 "and much more honest" — The pairing of a loss and a gain in one breath is the shape of the whole line.
7.19 "Nobody visits" — Blunt, monosyllabic, and set up to sound like a complaint.
7.20 "and asks about them" — The shared subject makes the two verbs one action; the sentence is still pretending to complain.
7.21 ", which was always the fear" — The relative clause reverses the meaning retroactively: what looked like loneliness was the thing being feared all along. Ending on the abstract noun keeps it dry.
8.1 "I took up swimming" — "Take up" = start a new pursuit; it usually implies a hobby and a certain earnestness.
8.2 "at an age" — "At an age when..." is a fixed frame for placing oneself in a demographic.
8.3 "when people take up complaining instead" — The repetition of "take up" with an incongruous object is the joke; "instead" completes the comparison.
8.4 "The first month" — A defined period, which lets the sentence be specific about a feeling.
8.5 "was humiliating" — The adjective is chosen for its size; nothing later in the line retracts it.
8.6 "in a way I'd forgotten was possible" — A relative clause with an embedded verb ("I'd forgotten [that it] was possible"). The Past Perfect places the forgetting before the swimming.
8.7 "Grown adults" — A slightly redundant phrase used deliberately: it emphasises the social expectation, not the age.
8.8 "are not often" — The negative plus a frequency adverb makes a claim about the world rather than the speaker.
8.9 "visibly bad at things in public" — "Bad at" + noun. "Visibly" and "in public" overlap on purpose; the redundancy mimics the awkwardness.
8.10 "There's an instructor" — Existential "there is" introduces a character who is never named and never speaks.
8.11 "who has watched me" — Present Perfect for observation continuing into the present; "watch somebody do something" takes the bare infinitive.
8.12 "fail to breathe correctly since March" — "Fail to + verb" is drier and more damning than "not be able to". "Since March" quantifies the patience.
8.13 "He has never once" — "Never once" is emphatic; it prepares a negative that will turn out to be a compliment.
8.14 "suggested" — The verb is deliberately mild, which makes the restraint being praised more precise.
8.15 "that I might be too old for this" — "Might" softens the unspoken thought; the whole sentence exists to record what was not said.
8.16 "That restraint" — A demonstrative pointing back at the previous sentence and naming its quality.
8.17 "is worth more to me" — "Be worth" + comparative, with the beneficiary marked by "to".
8.18 "than the technique" — The definite article makes technique the expected prize, which is what gives the preference its force.
8.19 "I'm now slow" — Present Simple for a current state, with the adjective placed bluntly.
8.20 "over a much longer distance" — "Over" for the extent covered; "much" strengthens the comparative.
8.21 ", which I'm choosing to call progress" — "Choose to call" admits the framing is a decision, not a fact. The self-awareness is what makes the boast likeable.

9.2 "is telling people things" — A gerund as complement; "tell somebody something" needs no preposition.
9.3 "they already know" — Contact relative clause; the flatness sets up the paradox of the next sentence.
9.4 "The other half" — The parallel structure promises symmetry, which the content then subverts.
9.5 "is being" — A gerund of "be", unusual and slightly awkward, which is precisely why it draws attention.
9.6 "the person who is allowed to say it" — A defining relative clause with a passive: the permission comes from elsewhere, and that is the point.
9.7 "Nobody pays" — The negative pronoun takes an affirmative verb, and putting it first makes the claim sound absolute.
9.8 "for the analysis" — "Pay for" names what the money buys; the definite article treats the analysis as the expected product.
9.9 "; they pay for the permission" — The semicolon holds two clauses in exact parallel; only the final noun changes, and that change is the thesis.
9.10 "This sounds cynical" — Pre-empting the reader's objection is a standard essayistic move; "sounds" concedes the impression without conceding the fact.
9.11 ", and for about a year" — The approximate duration keeps the confession from sounding rehearsed.
9.12 "it made me miserable" — "Make somebody + adjective" — a causative with no preposition.
9.13 "Then I noticed" — The narrative turn, marked by the plainest verb available.
9.14 "that the decisions actually changed" — "Actually" contrasts what happened with what was expected, not with the present moment.
9.15 ", which is more than most reports achieve" — A comparative clause with an ellipsis; the standard being beaten is set deliberately low.
9.16 "I've stopped pretending" — "Stop + -ing" for an abandoned behaviour, here an abandoned self-image.
9.17 "that the value" — The abstract noun with "the" treats value as a locatable object, which the next two elements then locate.
9.18 "is in the slides" — "Slides" for a presentation deck; the preposition "in" is what the sentence is arguing about.
9.19 "It's in the room" — The same preposition, a different noun, and the entire argument turns on that substitution.
9.20 ", and the slides" — The rejected term is picked up again for the final blow.
9.21 "are just what we do with our hands" — A free relative clause as complement. "Just" reduces the artefact to a gesture.
10.1 "Somebody asked me recently" — The vague subject keeps the focus on the question rather than the questioner.
10.2 "what I'd tell myself" — An embedded question containing a conditional; "would" here is hypothetical, not past.
10.3 "at twenty-five" — "At" + age, with the number standing alone for the self of that age.
10.4 "The honest answer" — Announcing honesty in advance signals that a less flattering answer is coming.
10.5 "is that he wouldn't have listened" — The shift from "I" to "he" for one's younger self is the central device of the whole line.
10.6 ", and he'd have been right not to" — Third conditional with ellipsis: "right not to [listen]". The negative infinitive at the end is what makes it a defence rather than a regret.
10.7 "Most of what I know now" — A free relative clause inside a quantifier phrase, fronted as the topic.
10.8 "I know because I ignored" — The verb is repeated rather than replaced, which gives the sentence its almost proverbial rhythm.
10.9 "exactly that kind of advice" — "That kind of" + singular noun; "exactly" points back at the question in the first sentence and closes the loop.
10.10 "The mistakes" — The definite article treats them as already known, though they are never described.
10.11 "were not detours" — The metaphor is introduced only to be denied, which is more efficient than explaining it.
10.12 "; they were the road" — The semicolon carries the reversal. The definite article in "the road" makes the claim absolute.
10.13 "That said" — A concessive discourse marker: everything before it stands, and is about to be qualified anyway.
10.14 ", I'd probably mention" — "Probably" undercuts the resolve, which is what keeps the sentence from sounding wise.
10.15 "the thing about sleep" — Studied vagueness. Naming it precisely would break the tone, and the reader supplies the content.
10.16 "He'd have laughed" — Third conditional again, now narrating an event that never happened as if remembered.
10.17 ", made a note" — Ellipsis of the auxiliary across a list of perfect verbs; the detail is small and devastating.
10.18 "and forgotten it by Thursday" — "By" + a day for a deadline. The arbitrary specificity of "Thursday" is what makes it funny.
10.19 "Which is, more or less" — A sentence fragment beginning with "which" — a deliberate breach of formal grammar used for exactly this closing effect.
10.20 ", what I did" — A free relative clause; the tense drops back to Past Simple as the hypothetical collapses into the real.
10.21 "with everything I was told" — A passive relative clause ending the line on the speaker himself, which quietly retracts any claim to have earned the wisdom.
