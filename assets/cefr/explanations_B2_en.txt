1.1 "We've been trying" — Present Perfect Continuous stresses the effort and the frustration, not the result. "We have tried" would suggest the attempts are over.
1.2 "to hire a second designer" — "Hire" is neutral business English; "recruit" is what the company does, "hire" is what a team does.
1.3 "since February" — "Since" + a point in time; "for" + a duration. The two are never interchangeable.
1.4 "Every candidate" — "Every" takes a singular verb even though we are thinking about many people.
1.5 "we actually liked" — A contact relative clause with "that" omitted. "Actually" here means "genuinely", not "at present" — a classic false friend.
1.6 "had already accepted something else" — Past Perfect: it happened before our decision. "Something else" politely avoids naming the competitor.
1.7 "HR keeps telling us" — "Keep + -ing" implies repetition to the point of irritation. "HR" is used as a singular department.
1.8 "the market is difficult" — Reported speech without "that" — normal in speech. Note "difficult", not "hard", in a business register.
1.9 ", which is probably true" — "Which" comments on the whole previous clause. "Probably" concedes the point while keeping distance.
1.10 "Still" — A concessive discourse marker: "even so". It signals that the concession above is about to be overturned.
1.11 "I suspect" — Hedging: it presents a firm opinion as a guess and so sounds less confrontational.
1.12 "our process is too slow" — "Too" = more than acceptable, always negative. The criticism is aimed at the process, not at people — deliberately.
1.13 "If we can't decide in under two weeks" — "In under" = in less than; common in business speech.
1.14 ", we shouldn't be surprised" — Understatement: it means "this is entirely our own fault".
1.15 "when people move on" — "Move on" = take another job. A softer, face-saving phrasal verb than "leave us".
2.1 "The report" — "The" marks it as the one document everyone in the conversation knows about.
2.2 "was supposed to be ready" — "Be supposed to" + the past tells you immediately that it was not ready.
2.3 "on Monday" — "On" + day. In a work context this normally means "by Monday".
2.4 "Nobody had mentioned" — Past Perfect for the missing information that existed before the problem appeared.
2.5 "that the data" — "Data" takes a singular verb in everyday and business English; the plural "data are" is academic.
2.6 "came from three different systems" — "Different" here means "separate", and it explains the whole problem in three words.
2.7 "Merging them" — A gerund as the subject of the sentence — compact and typical of written reports.
2.8 "took most of the week" — "Most of the" + a specific period. Note "took", not "lasted": the focus is on the cost in time.
2.9 ", and half the numbers still disagreed" — "Numbers disagree" is a light personification, standard in analytics talk. "Still" = despite all that work.
2.10 "In the end" — After difficulties. Compare "at the end of the week", which is only about time.
2.11 "we sent it out" — "Send out" implies distribution to a wider group, not to one person.
2.12 "with a note about the discrepancies" — "Discrepancy" is the formal word for numbers that do not match; the register lifts here because it is what the note said.
2.13 "To my surprise" — A fronted comment phrase; it prepares the reader for something unexpected.
2.14 ", nobody asked" — "Nobody" is already negative, so the verb stays affirmative.
2.15 "a single question about them" — "A single" adds emphasis after a negative: not even one. The irony is the point of the whole line.
3.1 "I've never been" — Present Perfect with "never" for a lifelong state that is still true.
3.2 "particularly good" — "Particularly" in a negative context is British understatement: it means "quite bad".
3.3 "at small talk" — "Good at + noun/-ing". "Small talk" is the fixed term for polite conversation about nothing.
3.4 "At conferences" — Plural with no article = conferences in general, whenever they happen.
3.5 "I tend to end up" — "Tend to" hedges a habit; "end up" suggests it was not a conscious plan.
3.6 "near the coffee machine" — In office humour the coffee machine is the standard symbol of avoiding people.
3.7 "Occasionally someone else" — "Occasionally" is weaker than "sometimes" and sets up the low expectations.
3.8 "starts the conversation" — "Start a conversation" is the natural collocation; "begin a conversation" sounds stiff.
3.9 ", and I meet someone genuinely interesting" — Adjectives after "someone" always follow the pronoun. "Genuinely" contrasts with the polite emptiness of small talk.
3.10 "More often" — A comparative used as a frequency marker; it quietly admits which outcome is normal.
3.11 "I just drink" — "Just" minimises the action and carries the self-deprecating tone.
3.12 "a lot of bad coffee" — Uncountable "coffee" with "a lot of". The joke lands on "bad".
3.13 "It's a strategy" — Calling it a strategy at all is ironic; the sentence knows it is not one.
3.14 "with obvious flaws" — "Flaw" = a weakness in a plan or design; "mistake" would be wrong here.
3.15 ", but it's the one I have" — Ellipsis of "strategy" after "the one". A dry, resigned ending.
4.1 "Our team" — A collective noun; British English allows a plural verb, American English prefers singular.
4.2 "switched to remote work" — "Switch to" = change completely and quickly.
4.3 "almost overnight" — Idiom: extremely fast, not literally in one night. "Almost" makes it credible.
4.4 "At first" — It sets up a contrast that the reader now expects to be broken.
4.5 "everyone said" — "Everyone" takes a singular verb but is often followed by "they" later.
4.6 "it was temporary" — Backshift in reported speech: "it is" becomes "it was".
4.7 "Two years later" — A time jump; the humour depends on this gap.
4.8 "the office is mostly used" — Passive: the office is not the actor. "Mostly" hedges without weakening the point.
4.9 "for meetings and free biscuits" — Bathos: pairing a serious noun with a trivial one is a standard English joke structure.
4.10 "The work itself" — The reflexive pronoun "itself" isolates the work from everything around it.
4.11 "hasn't really changed" — "Not really" is softer and more natural than "has not changed at all".
4.12 ", but the commute has disappeared" — "Commute" is the noun for the daily journey to work. Present Perfect: the result matters now.
4.13 "Nobody" — Placed first for emphasis, and again with an affirmative verb.
4.14 "I know" — A contact relative clause modifying "nobody"; it narrows the claim honestly.
4.15 "wants to go back" — Singular verb after "nobody". "Go back" here means "return to the old arrangement".
5.1 "My landlord" — The owner of the property you rent; "the owner" would sound distant.
5.2 "raised the rent" — "Raise" is transitive (he raised it); "rise" is intransitive (the rent rose).
5.3 "by ten per cent" — "By" + the size of the change. "To ten per cent" would mean something completely different.
5.4 "He explained" — "Explain" frames his words as reasonable, which the next sentences quietly undermine.
5.5 "that maintenance costs" — A compound noun: the first noun works like an adjective and stays singular in form.
5.6 "had gone up" — Past Perfect: the rise came before the conversation. "Go up" is the everyday alternative to "increase".
5.7 "The lift" — British; American English says "elevator".
5.8 "has been broken since November" — Present Perfect with a state adjective: it broke earlier and is still broken. "Since" marks the point at which the state began.
5.9 ", which slightly weakened his argument" — Understatement built with "slightly": the argument is in fact destroyed.
5.10 "I asked politely" — Naming your own politeness signals that the question was, in fact, pointed.
5.11 "whether the increase" — "Whether" is the correct form for an indirect yes/no question in a formal register.
5.12 "included fixing it" — After "include" the "-ing" form is obligatory.
5.13 "He said" — Plain reporting after all the careful hedging; the drop in register is deliberate.
5.14 "he would look into it" — "Would" is future-in-the-past inside reported speech; "look into" means "investigate", at least in theory.
5.15 ", which usually means no" — A dry gloss on the phrase "look into it", which is corporate English for polite refusal.
6.1 "I signed up" — "Sign up for" = commit publicly, often too quickly.
6.2 "for a marathon" — "A" because it is one race among many.
6.3 "in a moment of enthusiasm" — A fixed self-ironic frame: it means the decision was not thought through.
6.4 "Training" — Uncountable, no article; it names the whole ongoing activity.
6.5 "has been going" — Present Perfect Continuous for a process that started earlier and continues.
6.6 "better than expected" — Ellipsis of "than I had expected" — standard and idiomatic.
6.7 "The problem" — Announcing a problem after good news is a very common English rhythm.
6.8 "is that I now talk about running" — "The problem is that..." keeps the clause light; "now" marks the change.
6.9 "constantly" — Placed last for emphasis; it is the word that makes the sentence funny.
6.10 "My friends" — The victims of the previous sentence, introduced without comment.
6.11 "have started changing the subject" — "Change the subject" is the fixed idiom; "start" takes either "-ing" or "to".
6.12 "before I even begin" — "Even" intensifies: they act before there is anything to react to.
6.13 "Apparently" — Mock discovery: the speaker pretends to have just learned something obvious.
6.14 "there's a limit" — "There is a limit to how much..." is a set frame for polite complaint.
6.15 "to how much anyone can hear about split times" — "Split times" is running jargon; using it here is exactly the behaviour being mocked.
7.1 "The new system" — "The" because both speakers know which system; "new" is doing ironic work already.
7.2 "was introduced" — Agentless passive: nobody is named, which is the point.
7.3 "without much warning" — "Much" with an uncountable noun in a negative-flavoured phrase.
7.4 "We were told" — Passive of "tell": the speakers received the claim and did not make it.
7.5 "it would save" — "Would" as the future-in-the-past inside reported speech.
7.6 "everyone about an hour a day" — "Save somebody something" takes two objects. "A day" = per day.
7.7 "So far" — It marks an interim verdict, and it implies that the verdict may not improve.
7.8 "it has mostly generated" — "Generate" is corporate register; using it for meetings is the joke.
7.9 "new kinds of meetings" — "Kinds of" + plural. The absurdity is in "new kinds".
7.10 "To be fair" — A discourse marker introducing a concession to the other side.
7.11 "the interface" — The part of the system users actually see.
7.12 "is a genuine improvement" — "Genuine" signals that this praise, unlike the rest, is sincere.
7.13 "Whether that justifies" — A "whether" clause used as the subject: formal and slightly rhetorical.
7.14 "six months of migration" — "Migration" is the standard IT noun for moving data or users to a new system.
7.15 "is another question" — Idiom for a polite refusal to agree; it closes the argument without stating a verdict.
8.1 "My neighbour" — British spelling; American English writes "neighbor".
8.2 "decided to learn the trumpet" — Instruments take "the" after "learn" and "play".
8.3 "at fifty-two" — "At" + age; the age is what makes the story worth telling.
8.4 "For the first month" — "For" + duration with "the first" specifying which month.
8.5 "it was, frankly" — "Frankly" between commas is a comment on how the speaker is speaking, not on the event.
8.6 ", quite hard to listen to" — The preposition stays at the end: "hard to listen to". British "quite" here weakens rather than intensifies.
8.7 "I nearly said something" — "Nearly" + past = it did not happen. "Say something" is the idiom for making a complaint.
8.8 ", and then I remembered" — The turn of the anecdote; the conjunction carries the whole change of attitude.
8.9 "how badly I sing" — An exclamative clause with normal word order: "how badly I sing", never "how badly do I sing".
8.10 "He's improved enormously" — "Improve" is intransitive here — no object needed.
8.11 "since the summer" — "Since" + a point in time again; "the summer" is a shared reference.
8.12 ", and I'm oddly proud of him" — "Oddly" admits the feeling is disproportionate, which makes it warm rather than sentimental.
8.13 "Now" — A short marker closing the time loop opened by "For the first month".
8.14 "the silence on Sundays" — "On Sundays" = every Sunday.
8.15 "feels almost strange" — "Feel" + adjective. "Almost" keeps the sentence from tipping into sentimentality — the classic English brake.
9.1 "I don't usually" — The frequency adverb goes after the auxiliary in a negative sentence.
9.2 "trust productivity advice" — "Advice" is uncountable: never "advices", never "an advice".
9.3 ", mostly because it all contradicts itself" — "It all" refers back to the uncountable "advice", so the verb is singular.
9.4 "One book" — "One" instead of "a" sets up a contrast with the next book.
9.5 "tells you to start" — "Tell somebody to do something". "You" is impersonal here, meaning people in general.
9.6 "with the hardest task" — "Start with" = begin from. The superlative takes "the".
9.7 "The next one" — "One" avoids repeating "book".
9.8 "insists" — "Insist" adds a note of stubbornness; it prepares the contradiction.
9.9 "you should build momentum first" — "Build momentum" is the standard collocation in self-help writing, quoted here with visible distance.
9.10 "What has actually worked for me" — A pseudo-cleft: fronting the "what" clause creates suspense before the answer.
9.11 "is much less exciting" — "Much" strengthens the comparative "less exciting".
9.12 ": fewer tabs and an earlier alarm" — The colon delivers the anticlimax. "Fewer" with countable nouns; "less" would be wrong.
9.13 "Nobody" — Affirmative verb after the negative pronoun, as elsewhere.
9.14 "is going to publish" — "Going to" for a confident prediction based on the evidence just given.
9.15 "a book about that" — The flat ending is the punchline: the honest answer is unsellable.
10.1 "She'd been warning us" — Past Perfect Continuous: repeated warnings before the events that follow.
10.2 "about that deadline" — "Warn somebody about something". "That" points to a deadline both sides remember.
10.3 "for weeks" — "For" + duration; the plural suggests it went on far too long.
10.4 "We nodded" — A physical detail standing in for false agreement.
10.5 "in every meeting" — "Every" + singular noun for each occasion without exception.
10.6 "and changed nothing" — The bluntness after "nodded" is where the irony sits.
10.7 "When the client" — "When" + past for the trigger event in a narrative.
10.8 "moved the date forward" — In British usage this normally means "made it earlier" — the direction that causes panic.
10.9 ", the panic was almost impressive" — Ironic praise: "impressive" is doing the opposite of its usual job.
10.10 "Somehow" — It admits the speaker cannot explain the success, and refuses to take credit.
10.11 "we delivered" — "Deliver" without an object is standard project English: we produced what was promised.
10.12 "on the Friday" — "The" before a day refers to a specific day inside the story already told.
10.13 "Not once" — A negative adverbial fronted for emphasis; it forces inversion in the next part.
10.14 "has she said \"I told you so\"" — The inversion "has she said" is required after a fronted negative. The quoted idiom names the temptation she resisted.
10.15 ", which is why everyone listens to her now" — "Which is why" links the whole story to its moral without stating it directly.
